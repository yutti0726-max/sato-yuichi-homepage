A Diving Equipment Trouble Story | The Importance of Trusting Your Instructor

Updated: September 7, 2026

I've had an experience where an equipment malfunction briefly cut off my breathing underwater. Facing that situation, what mattered above everything else was "trusting my instructor and following their direction." Here's that experience, plus the basic response to running out of air that's taught in courses.

In short, the best response to trouble underwater is not trying to solve it alone, and instead trusting the basic actions you learned in your course and your instructor's direction.

What Actually Happened?

I once had an experience where an equipment malfunction briefly cut off my ability to breathe underwater. Even knowing intellectually that I needed to stay calm, the moment your breath is actually cut off is genuinely alarming. What kept it from turning into a serious accident was, I believe, the basic actions I'd learned in courses and the instructor who was there with me.

What Actually Helped in That Moment?

When the trouble happened, the biggest help in regaining my composure wasn't trying to panic and fix it myself, but following my instructor's signals and direction. Underwater, panicking and acting erratically is the most dangerous thing you can do. Being able to make the call to "not solve it alone" and instead "follow the direction of someone trained" was, I believe, ultimately the safest response.

Trouble underwater can happen to anyone. What matters isn't just "avoiding trouble" — it's having thought through "how will I respond if it happens" beforehand.

What's the Basic Response to "Running Out of Air" Taught in Courses?

In PADI's Open Water course, the basic response to running out of air or breathing trouble is taught early on.

  1. Stay calm: don't panic — first maintain a posture that lets you breathe
  2. Signal: signal your buddy or instructor with the "out of air" hand signal, a hand placed against your throat
  3. Use the octopus: secure your breathing from your buddy's backup regulator (octopus)
  4. Ascend calmly: staying close to your buddy, ascend at a controlled speed without ever holding your breath

This whole sequence is something you practice repeatedly during your course until it's ingrained into muscle memory. Not just knowing it intellectually, but having your body move on its own, is what makes the difference when real trouble happens.

What Can I Do Day to Day to Avoid Panicking?

  • Regularly review basic skills: if you've had a gap since your last dive, take a refresher course
  • Get comfortable handling your own gear: the more dives you log using your own gear rather than rentals, the smoother your response to trouble becomes
  • Tell your instructor about any concerns before diving: be honest about how you're feeling or anything on your mind
  • Don't plan a dive beyond your means: choose points and dive counts that match your own experience and stamina

Going through this experience myself is part of what made me want to take Rescue Diver, to learn emergency response more systematically.

Summary

Equipment trouble underwater is never something that only happens to someone else. That's exactly why properly internalizing the basic response taught in courses, and trusting your instructor and trained knowledge when it counts, is the best way to protect yourself.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. What should I do first if I run out of air underwater?

A. First stay calm, and signal your buddy or instructor with the "out of air" hand signal, a hand placed against your throat. Then secure your breathing from your buddy's octopus (backup regulator).

Q. How do I avoid panicking?

A. Rather than trying to solve it alone, following the direction of a trained instructor or your buddy ends up being the safest approach. Reviewing basic skills regularly also helps.

Q. Who should take a refresher course?

A. It's recommended for anyone who's had a gap since their last dive. Re-ingraining basic skills into muscle memory lets you respond calmly if real trouble happens.

Q. Is equipment trouble common?

A. It doesn't happen often, but it can happen to anyone. What matters is having thought through how you'd respond beforehand.

Q. How did this experience lead to taking Rescue Diver?

A. Going through this made me want to learn emergency response more systematically, which became one of the reasons I decided to take Rescue Diver.
