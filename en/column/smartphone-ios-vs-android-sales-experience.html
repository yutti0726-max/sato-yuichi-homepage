Why I Recommend Android Over iPhone | Lessons From Selling Phones and Using Them Myself

Updated: September 13, 2026

I replace my main phone almost every year and always carry three at once. I've also sold phones professionally, recommending devices to every kind of customer. Plenty of articles compare iOS and Android in the abstract — here's what I've actually concluded, from the sales floor and from years of side-by-side use.

In short: the OS itself and the way Apple devices work together is genuinely better on iPhone. But once you factor in app quality, app price, and hardware variety, Android comes out ahead in my experience.

Why do I recommend Android on the sales floor?

On the sales floor, customers span every age and every use case, and choosing by brand image alone often leads to regret later — "this isn't what I expected." Having replaced my own phone every year and run three devices side by side, I don't think iPhone is bad. I recommend Android more often because there's simply more you can do for free, and a wider range of hardware to choose from.

What does iPhone genuinely do better?

To be fair up front: iOS itself is polished. I used to be a full "iPhone person" myself — an iPhone Pro Max, an iPad Pro 13" with the Nano-texture glass option, and an Apple Watch Ultra. The way those devices talk to each other, along with a MacBook, is still genuinely excellent. If you want your entire digital life unified around Apple, that integration is worth a lot.

So why do I still recommend Android?

Because any manufacturer can build hardware on top of Android, individual Android phones end up with real personality. Xiaomi partners with Bose for speaker tuning and with Leica for camera work. OPPO partners with Hasselblad and ships cameras that beat iPhone in some respects. Apple's everything-in-house approach has its own appeal, but the sharp, specialist collaborations you see on Android simply don't exist on iPhone.

Why is there such a gap in app quality and quantity?

The other big factor is the economics of app development. Registering as an Android developer costs $25, once. An iOS developer account costs $99 — every year. Add in the fact that Android holds roughly 70% of the global smartphone OS market, and it's obvious why developers worldwide build for Android first and often price aggressively.

I see this firsthand in PDF editing, something I deal with constantly for work. iPhone's best PDF apps tend to be subscription-based, while Android has several free apps with real functionality. I go into this specific comparison in Editing PDFs on your phone: why iPhone means a subscription and Android is free — worth a read if this resonates.

Some people assume "Android" means "cheap." That's not quite right. Entry-level Android phones are cheap, sure, but chase flagship specs and Android — or a Windows laptop to match — can easily cost more than the equivalent Apple hardware. The real strength of Android isn't the price tag, it's the breadth of specs and apps on offer.

So which should you actually choose?

If you want iPad, Apple Watch, and MacBook all tied together, I'll still honestly recommend iPhone — that integration is worth it. But if you care more about hardware personality from specialist manufacturer collaborations, the sheer number of free high-quality apps, and a wider range of specs to choose from, Android deserves serious consideration. I cover my own move from a full Apple setup to an Android-and-Windows one in I used to be an iPhone loyalist — here's why I switched to Android + Windows.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. So which is actually better, iPhone or Android?

A. If you want everything unified with iPad, Apple Watch, and MacBook, iPhone is genuinely comfortable. But if you care about app quality and quantity, how much you can do for free, and hardware variety, Android has the edge in my experience.

Q. Why are there more free, high-quality apps on Android?

A. Registering as an Android developer costs $25 once. iOS costs $99 every single year. Android also holds roughly 70% of the global OS market. Put those together and developers worldwide build for Android first, which means more free, high-quality apps end up there.

Q. Is there anything iPhone genuinely does better?

A. The polish of the OS itself, and the seamless integration across iPad, Apple Watch, and MacBook, are real strengths I noticed after using them for years. If you want your whole life built around Apple devices, that's a legitimate reason to stay.

Q. What kind of differences exist between Android manufacturers?

A. Because any manufacturer can build hardware on Android, specialist collaborations happen often — Xiaomi partners with Bose for sound and Leica for cameras, OPPO partners with Hasselblad for cameras. That kind of hardware personality doesn't exist on iPhone.

Q. Is Android actually cheaper than iPhone?

A. Entry-level Android is certainly cheaper. But chase flagship specs and Android — or a Windows laptop to match — can easily cost more than an iPhone. The real strength of Android isn't low price, it's the breadth of specs and apps available.
