Which Parts Actually Matter in a Custom PC? | What a 3-Way SLI Build Taught Me About GPU Limits and Why CPU/Memory Matter More

Updated: September 7, 2026

I once ran a "3-way SLI" setup with three high-end graphics cards stacked together. Drawing on that experience, here's a candid look at the limits of spending on graphics cards, and why CPU and memory are actually what matter most for everyday comfort.

In short, a GPU only needs to meet the recommended specs for the games you want to play — CPU and memory are what more directly shape your everyday comfort.

Is More GPU Always Better?

I once ran a "3-way SLI" setup with three high-end graphics cards. The short version: stacking that much GPU had only a marginal impact on gaming, and honestly, it didn't go much beyond "self-satisfaction." The reason is simple — developers have no reason to build a game that only runs comfortably on that kind of extreme setup. Games are made to run on the environment most players actually have, so an extremely over-specced build produces only a barely noticeable difference in practice.

A graphics card just needs to meet the recommended specs for the games you actually want to play — that's the conclusion I reached through this experience. Wanting more than that is, in my view, more about hobby and self-satisfaction than actual performance gain.

Why Do CPU and Memory Shape Everyday Use So Much?

CPU and memory, on the other hand, are extremely important. GPU performance matters for specific tasks like video encoding, but for "everything else you do every day," it's the CPU and memory that have a direct impact.

  • App launch speed: CPU and memory performance translates directly into how fast things feel
  • How light your browser feels: the more tabs you keep open, the more you're affected by memory capacity
  • Comfort when running multiple tasks at once: the more multitasking-heavy your workflow, the more directly memory shortage translates into a sluggish feel
  • Power consumption: CPU generation and efficiency can significantly change power draw for the same workload

A graphics card is "a part that matters for specific uses (gaming, encoding)," while CPU and memory are "parts that matter in every scenario of using a PC." If you want to raise your everyday comfort across the board, I believe you should prioritize investing in CPU and memory first.

If your budget is limited when choosing parts, it helps to first map out how you actually use your PC day to day. If you do a lot of video editing or encoding, prioritize the GPU; if you multitask or run several apps at once, prioritize memory and CPU. It makes a big difference to how satisfied you feel day to day.

Summary

A graphics card only needs to meet the recommended specs for the games you want to play — stacking more beyond that produces only a barely noticeable difference, which is the real conclusion I reached from running three of them. CPU and memory, on the other hand, are high-return parts that directly shape comfort in every scenario of using a PC. If you're stuck on which parts to choose, I'd recommend going back to how you actually use your PC and setting your priorities from there.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Should I buy a high-end graphics card?

A. It's enough to meet the recommended specs for the games you actually want to play. Beyond that, the difference you can feel is minor, and it shifts from performance gain into self-satisfaction territory.

Q. If my budget is limited, what should I prioritize?

A. If you do a lot of video editing or encoding, prioritize the GPU; if you multitask or run several apps at once, prioritize memory and CPU. It makes a big difference to how satisfied you feel day to day.

Q. How much memory do I need to feel comfortable?

A. The ideal amount depends on how you use your PC, but the more often you run multiple tasks at once, the more directly a lack of memory translates into a sluggish feel.

Q. Is there a benefit to a multi-card setup like 3-way SLI?

A. Having actually built and tested it, stacking that much GPU had only a marginal impact on gaming, with a barely noticeable difference in practice.

Q. Which affects everyday operation more, CPU or GPU?

A. The CPU (and memory) is what affects nearly every scenario of using a PC — launching apps, browser behavior, multitasking comfort. The GPU is a part that matters for specific use cases.
