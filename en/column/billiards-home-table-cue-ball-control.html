Installing a Billiard Table at Home | Cue Ball Control Is the Foundation

Updated: September 8, 2026

I love billiards enough that I installed a Brunswick table on the second floor of my own home. From setting up the table to daily maintenance and the cue-ball-control fundamentals improvement is built on, here's what I've learned from real experience.

In short, improving at billiards comes down to controlling where the cue ball goes, and if you're setting up a table at home, cloth and shaft maintenance are the foundation everything else is built on.

What do you need to set up a billiard table at home?

I bought a Brunswick "Metro" — a model I understand was used at the 2006 All Japan Championship — and installed it on the second floor of my home. Since it's a wooden house, I reinforced the table's legs beforehand so the floor could handle the weight. I also put together a full set of accessories: a billiard hanger (cue rack), my own personal cue — an Exceed Cue EXC-210N — and a proper shaft.

What should I watch for when choosing a personal cue and shaft?

A shaft must never be warped. A warped shaft won't send the cue ball where you're aiming, and it gets in the way of improving. That makes how you store it critical — I'm always conscious of keeping it in a position and orientation that keeps it perfectly straight. Keeping a cover on it so it doesn't collect dust also matters.

If you're setting up a billiard table at home, always check the load-bearing capacity of the spot beforehand. Especially on the second floor of a wooden house, reinforcing the table's legs is a basic precondition for enjoying it safely.

What maintenance is essential for a home table?

Above everything, if you're keeping a table at home, cloth maintenance matters most. Even small amounts of dust and debris gradually accumulate and start affecting how the balls roll. Keeping a cover on it when not in use to block dust, and regularly checking the cloth's condition, are the basics for keeping it enjoyable to play on.

What is cue-ball control, the foundation of improvement?

Billiards is a sport of moving balls. The very first thing to build is control over where you send the cue ball. I've been working through improvement in roughly this order:

  1. Learn to strike the dead center of the cue ball cleanly and vertically
  2. Learn how much spin different contact points on the cue ball produce
  3. Learn where the ball goes when that spin hits the cushion

Even a small change in spin and force can change the ball's direction dramatically. That's exactly why I think it's a sport that rewards deep, patient repetition.

Summary

What installing a billiard table at home really taught me is that the foundation of improvement is keeping the table itself in good condition. Stay on top of cloth and shaft maintenance, and start by practicing a clean, vertical hit on the center of the cue ball — from there, controlling spin and cushion rebounds comes together gradually too.

FAQ

Q. What should I watch out for when installing a billiard table at home?

A. The load-bearing capacity of the installation spot. Especially on the second floor of a wooden house, you need to check the floor's strength and reinforce the table's legs as needed.

Q. How often does the cloth need maintenance?

A. Even small amounts of dust and debris affect how the balls roll as they build up, so regular care plus protecting the table with a cover are both essential.

Q. Should a beginner own a personal cue?

A. It's not mandatory, but having a cue and shaft that fit your own grip does change how fast you improve, in my experience.

Q. What matters most in shaft care?

A. Storing it so it never warps. A warped shaft directly hurts your shot accuracy.

Q. What should I practice first to improve at billiards?

A. Being able to strike the dead center of the cue ball cleanly and vertically. Without that, controlling spin afterward never stabilizes.

Q. How do you learn to control cue-ball spin?

A. Once you've built accuracy hitting dead center, you gradually learn, through repetition, how the contact point (up, down, left, or right) changes the amount of spin and how that spin changes the rebound angle off the cushion.
